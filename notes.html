Option 1

Traditional in-class exams: one or two midterms and a final.

In-class quizzes for basic knowledge. An adaptation of the thing I tried for 201, where you can try a few times, and you need to get 100% to get credit. Maybe something like: you have 3 in-class attempts; after that you need to arrange to get it done outside of class. If I do this, I need to figure out other activities for folks to do who have already passed the quiz on the first or second attempt. Also need to decide exactly when these attempts happen: once a week?

“Homework” that we grade together and discuss in class.

Independent research assignment: find a PDF of a paper related to our readings, read it, then what? Asking for a written summary invites AI. Print it out, read and annotate by hand, and turn in a copy with your handwritten notes? Lead in-class discussion, or small group discussion?

Papers? Option 1: make them not for any credit, only feedback. Option 2: a few traditional short analysis papers for credit, and don’t worry about AI. Option 3: In class argument analysis exercises of graduated difficulty.

Creative thinking techniques: incorporate ideas from Weston (2007)

Option 2

Ungrading of some sort.

See [[daily/2026-05-04]] for Michaela’s version which sounds workable.

She divided the course into four “topics”. At the end of each topic, there was an in-class writing day, where the students write an open-handout/notes essay based on one of several prompts. So kind of like an exam. These essays received feedback but no grades. Nothing else received written feedback.

Along with this, there were short in-class writing tasks that were turned in but not graded.

She also did in-class “co-teaching” days. She had 18 students. Divided them into pairs and had 9 co-taught days, where she would meet with the students one week ahead, and they would come up with a plan together about how to lead discussion on a topic.

So: 30 days - 4 writing days = 26 days of instruction.

I think the idea would have to be that the “co-teaching” days are spread out. So a few weeks to get started, followed by every other class being a co-teaching day, more or less.

Topics

Include something about language and law. See Marmor and Soames (2011) for some possibilities. (rosen2011?) is about textualism vs intentionalism, might fit well with stuff on speaker meaning vs. semantic meaning, and theories of meaning more generally. Endicott (1997) (endicott2011?) Endicott (2001) are all possible sources for a discussion of vagueness in the law (pair with Cecco (2026)). Could also be paired with Nguyen (2021), but that drifts away from phil language.

With that in mind, perhaps I should make an effort to include optional recommended readings that “drift away” from phil language—that is, readings that connect our topics to other topics in other areas. I don’t think I would want those to be our assigned readings—we need to stay on topic.
