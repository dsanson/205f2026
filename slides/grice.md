# The Phenomena

## 'And' and '$\wedge$'

In logic, we introduce a sentential operator, '$\wedge$', say that it means 'and', and assign it the following truth table:

   $p$   $q$   $p \wedge q$
  ----- ----- --------------
    T     T         T
    T     F         F
    F     T         F
    F     F         F

This implies that '$p \wedge q$' and '$q \wedge p$' mean the same thing.

## {.center}

*Conversational implicature*
:   When you say one thing, but communicate something else (or something more).

---

> Sally: "What's the weather going to be like today?" Harold: "You should
    bring your umbrella."

What does Harold say? What does he communicate?

---

> A: "How is Charles getting on in his new job?" B: "Quite well, I think: he likes
 his colleagues, and he hasn't been to prison yet."

What does B say? What does B communicate?

---

A basic observation about how we use language: we often **communicate** more than what we **say**.

Call these additional things that we communicate **conversational implicatures**.

# The Theory

## Cooperation {.center}

Conversation is a cooperative venture, aimed at communication. Conversational implicatures are generated by *apparent violations* of the
cooperative norms that govern conversation.

---

The Cooperative Principle
:   Make your conversational contribution such as required, at the stage at
    which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange
    in which you are engaged.

## The Four Maxims {.smaller}

Quality
:   Try to make your contribution one that is true: (1) do not say what you
believe to be false; (2) do not say that for which you lack adequate evidence.

Quantity
:   \(1) Make your contribution as informative as is required; (2) Do not make
your contribution more informative than is required.

Relation
:   Be relevant.

Manner
:   Be perspicuous: (1) avoid obscurity of expression; (2) avoid ambiguity; (3) be brief; (4) be orderly.

---

    bring your umbrella."

## {.center}

\ 
:   Someone who says $p$ **implicates** $q$ if:

    1.  It is appropriate to assume that she is observing the conversational
        maxims and the Cooperative Principle.
    2.  Her saying $p$ is consistent with the maxims and the principle only if
        we suppose that she also believes $q$.
    3.  The speaker thinks (and thinks the hearer thinks that the speaker thinks)
        that the hearer can work out that $q$ is what is required to make $p$
        relevant.

Calculability
:   Conversational implicatures can be calculated on the basis of *what is said* and the *maxims*.

---

Nondetachability
:   If saying *S* in context *C* gives rise to implicature *I*, and *T* is another way
    of saying the same thing as *S*, then saying *T* in context *C* would also give
    rise to implicature *I*. 

Except for implicatures that rely on the maxim of manner!

This implies that the implicature is a function of what is said, not something else.

Cancelability
:    Conversational implicatures can be cancelled.

---

Indeterminacy
:   Conversational implicatures are often indeterminate, since there might be various possible things someone might mean by what they say in the conversational context.

---

Conventional Implicatures
:   Over time, certain conversational implicatures may become conventional, and perhaps even become part of the meaning of what is said.
