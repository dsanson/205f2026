# Historical Context

## Traditional Arguments

The Traditional Conservative Argument Against Pornography
:   Pornography should be banned because it is obscene and morally corrupting.

The Traditional Liberal Defense of a Right to Pornography
:   The state should not interfere with pornography, for three reasons: (1) to
    do so would violate freedom of speech; (2) to do so would violate the
    right to privacy; (3) pornography is relatively harmless.

## Indianapolis Ordinance

In 1984, Indianapolis passes an ordinance, drafted by Catherine MacKinnon, that
defines pornography as a civil rights violation against women, allowing women to
sue pornographers for civil damages.

## American Booksellers Inc vs. Hudnut

In 1985, the 7th Circuit Court of Appeals (the one in Chicago), throws out the
Indianapolis Ordinance. (The opinion was written by Judge Frank Easterbrook.)

## MacKinnon's Argument

Pornography should be banned because it violates the civil rights of women: (1)
it violates their right to equal status; (2) it violates their right to free
speech (by silencing them).

# Langton

## Three Kinds of Speech Act

Locutionary Act
:   The act *of* saying something.

Illocutionary Act
:   The act you perform *in* saying something.

Perlocutionary Act
:   The act you perform *by* saying something.

## Speech Acts and Free Speech

Easterbrook agrees with MacKinnon that pornography is an act that depicts
subordination and tends to perpetuate subordination. But he throws out the
ordinance on free speech grounds:

> "The First Amendment means that government has no power to restrict
> expression because of its message \[or\] its ideas."

But this involves a limited view about the uses of language.

---

> Pornography is not always done with words. Yet Easterbrook's description
exemplifies the tendency of which Austin complained. Pornography depicts
subordination and causes it. That---in Austin's terms---is to describe its
locutionary and perlocutionary dimensions. What is missing is a description
of the actions constituted by pornographic utterances: in Austin's terms,
pornography's *illocutionary* force. MacKinnon supplies such a description
when she says that pornography is an act of subordination. (Langton, 296)

## Subordination and Silencing

Subordination
:   Pornography is an act of subordinating women.

Langton argues that this is the *illocutionary* act performed by the pornographer.


Silencing
:   Pornography silences women.

Langton argues that this is the *perlocutionary* act performed by the pornographer.

## Subordination as Illocutionary Act

Is subordinating one of the things I can do *in* saying something?

-   *hurting* is perlocutionary: 'I shoot her' and you shoot her.
-   *oppressing* is perlocutionary: I say something racist, and thereby perpetuate racism.
-   but *subordinating* is illocutionary.

---

:::displayed-sentence
Blacks are not permitted to vote. (as uttered by a legislator in South
Africa enacting apartheid.)

Whites only. (as posted on the door of a restaurant)
:::

---

To subordinate a group is to:

1.  They unfairly *rank* members in that group as having inferior worth...
2.  ...in a way that *legitimates* discrimationary behavior...
3.  ...and unjustly *deprives* members of the group of important powers.

---

Questions:

1.  Does the pornographer have the right sort of authority to subordinate via pornographic speech? (this is about felicity conditions---analogy to the South African legislator)
2.  Does the pornographer's speech act have the correct uptake to count as illocutionary subordination?
3.  Does pornography in fact have the perlocutionary effect of  unjustly depriving a group of important powers?


## Silencing 

> If you are powerful, you sometimes have the ability to silence the speech of the powerless. One way might be to stop the powerless from speaking at all. Gag them, threaten them, condemn them to solitary confinement. But there is another, less dramatic but equally effective, way. Let them speak. Let them say whatever they like to whomever they like, but stop that speech from [...] counting as the action it was intended to be. Some speech acts are unspeakable for women in some contexts: although the appropriate words can be uttered, those utterances fail to count as the actions they were intended to be.

---

Three kinds of silencing:

-   locutionary: make it so that someone does not speak (e.g., through intimidation or gagging)
-   perlocutionary frustration: your speech act does not have the intended effect on your audience.
-   illocutionary disablement:  "although the appropriate words are uttered, with the appropriate intention, the speaker fails to perform the intended illocutionary act."

---

> Example (i): Warning. This example is from Donald Davidson. Imagine this: the actor is acting a scene in which there is supposed to be a fire.... It is his role to imitate as persuasively as he can a man who is trying to warn others of a fire. "Fire!" he screams. And perhaps he adds, at the behest of the author, "I mean it! Look at the smoke !" etc. And now a real fire breaks out, and the actor tries vainly to warn the real audience. "Fire!" he screams. "I mean it! Look at the smoke!" etc.
