# Some Background Epistemology

## Empiricism and Rationalism

Empiricism
:   Sense experience is the only legitimate source of knowledge.

Rationalism
:   Reason is also a legitimate source of knowledge.

## An Argument for Rationalism

:::{.argument}
1.  Our knowledge of logic and mathematics comes from reason, not sense experience.
2.  Experimental science presupposes logic and mathematics.
3.  So, the knowledge we gain from experimental science has at its source not just sense experience, but also reason.
:::

# Logical Empiricism

## The Reduction of Math to Logic

Frege and Russell both thought we could reduce mathematics to logic.
With the right definitions, claims like,

1.   $2 + 2 = 4$, 

reduce to claims like, 

2.  If $P$ then $P$.

(This project ran into unexpected difficulty. The current consensus is that no such reduction is possible.)

## Logical Truths

Contrast:

1.  The sun is out today in Normal, IL
2.  If it rains then it rains. 

Neither is nonsense. So (following Frege) they both express a sense or thought.

---

1.  The sun is out today in Normal, IL
2.  If it rains then it rains. 

The sense of (1) determines different truth values in different circumstances. That is, the truth of (1) depends not just on the sense, but also on the world.

---

1.  The sun is out today in Normal, IL
2.  If it rains then it rains. 

The sense of (2) determines the same truth value, true, in every possible circumstance. That is, the truth of (2) depends only on the sense, not the world.

## Wittgenstein on Tautologies

2.  If it rains then it rains. 

Logical truths like (2) are *tautologies*. They are not *nonsense*, but they have no *substantive* content.

They are true in virtue of meaning (sense) alone.

## Analytic Truths

\(3) is not a logical truth:

3.   Every bachelor is unmarried.

But, if we "unpack" the meaning of 'bachelor', we get,

4.   Every unmarried man is unmarried.

And (4) *is* a logical truth.

---

Analytic truths *reduce* to logical truths, and so are also tautologies.

The truth of an analytic truth depends on (a) the definitions of any non-basic terms, like 'bachelor' and (b) logic.

## Logical Empiricism: a Vision

Language makes contact with experience in the form of basic observation sentences, e.g.,

-   This is red.

'This' refers to some immediate object of sense experience, and 'is red' categorizes it by how it appears.

---

More abstract sentences, like,

-   Fire Engines are red.

reduce, via definition, to basic observation sentences.

---

This leaves a residue of sentences that are no basic observation sentences and do not reduce to basic observation sentences.

These are either nonsense (metaphysics and theology) or analytic (mathematics).

## The Two Dogmas {-}

> One [dogma] is a belief in some fundamental cleavage between truths which are
> *analytic*, or grounded in meanings independently of matters of fact, and
> truth which are *synthetic*, or grounded in fact.

> The other dogma is reductionism: the belief that each meaningful statement is
> equivalent to some logical construct upon terms which refer to immediate
> experience.

## Intension/extension

-  The *intension* of a name is the sense it expresses (its "meaning").
-  The *extension* of a name is its referent.
-  The *intension* of a general term or predicate the sense it expresses (its "meaning").
-  The *extension* of a general term or predicate is the class of all entities that it is true of.

# Skepticism About Meanings

## What are they? {.scrollable}

> For the theory of meaning the most conspicuous question is as to  the nature of its objects: what sort of things are meanings? They are  evidently intended to be ideas, somehow --- mental ideas for some  semanticists, Platonic ideas for others. Objects of either sort are so  elusive, not to say debatable, that there seems little hope of erecting  a fruitful science about them. It is not even clear, granted meanings,  when we have two and when we have one; it is not clear when linguistic forms should be regarded as synonymous, or alike in meaning,  and when they should not. If a standard of synonymy should be arrived at, we may reasonably expect that the appeal to meanings as  entities will not have played a very useful part in the enterprise. (22)

# Analyticity as Truth by Definition

## Proposal

> There are those who find it soothing to say that the analytic statements of the second class reduce to those of the first class, the logical  truths, by definition; 'bachelor', e.g., is defined as 'unmarried man'. But how do we find that 'bachelor' is defined as 'unmarried man'? (24)

## Not Dictionary Definitions

> The lexicographer is an  empirical scientist, whose business is the recording of antecedent facts;  and if he glosses 'bachelor' as 'unmarried man' it is because of his belief  that there is a relation of synonymy between these forms, implicit in  general or preferred usage prior to his own work. [...] . Certainly the "definition" which  is the lexicographer's report of an observed synonymy cannot be taken  as the ground of the synonymy (24)

## Dictionary Definitions and Usage

> Just what it means to affirm synonymy, just what the interconnections may be which are necessary and sufficient in order that two linguistic forms be properly describable as synonymous, is far from  clear; but, whatever these interconnections may be, ordinarily they  are grounded in usage. Definitions reporting selected instances of  synonymy come then as reports upon usage. (25)

:::notes
So in this case, what we have are reports of facts about usage. Are there facts about usage that determine when two words are synonymous? What might those be?
:::

## Definitions as Explications

> In explication the purpose is not merely to paraphrase the  definiendum into an outright synonym, but actually to improve upon  the definiendum by refining or supplementing its meaning.

Examples: 

-   the biologist's explication of 'fish', so that it does not include whales.
-   Frege's explication of 'meaning', which introduces the distinction between two kinds of meaning, sense and reference.

---

> Such a definition still owes its explicative function to pre-existing synonymies (25)

Do you see why?

## Definitions as Conventional Abbreviation

Quine goes on about this at some length. I'll give you an example on the board, of a language that just has 'or' and 'not' as its primitives, and defines 'and' and 'if...then...' as abbreviations for sentences containing only 'or' and 'not'.

## Definitions as Conventional Abbreviation {.smaller .scrollable}

> But these correlations are not arbitrary. They are supposed to show  how the primitive notations can accomplish all purposes, save brevity  and convenience, of the redundant language. Hence the definiendum  and its definiens may be expected, in each case, to be related in one  or another of the three ways lately noted. The definiens may be a  faithful paraphrase of the definiendum into the narrower notation,  preserving a direct synonymy as of antecedent usage; or the definiens  may, in the spirit of explication, improve upon the antecedent usage  of the definiendum; or finally, the definiendum may be a newly created  notation, newly endowed with meaning here and now. (27)

Only the last case plausibly gives rise to a *new* synonymy.



# Interchangeability

## Proposal

> A natural suggestion, deserving close examination, is that the  synonymy of two linguistic forms consists simply in their interchangeability in all contexts without change of truth value; interchangeability, in Leibniz's phrase, *salva veritate*. (27)

-   Note that this is Frege's test for co-reference.
-   But if we include indirect contexts, it is also a test for sameness of sense.

## Some Problems {.smaller .scrollable}

>Truths which become false under substitution of 'unmarried man' for 'bachelor' are  easily constructed with help of 'bachelor of arts' or 'bachelor's buttons'. Also with help of quotation, thus:  'Bachelor' has less than ten letters.  Such counterinstances can, however, perhaps be set aside by treating  the phrases 'bachelor of arts' and 'bachelor's buttons' and the quotation ''bachelor'' each as a single indivisible word and then stipulating that the interchangeability salva veritate which is to be the  touchstone of synonymy is not supposed to apply to fragmentary occurrences inside of a word. ## Modal Contexts

Here is a schema for testing for synonymy of 'A' and 'B':

4.  Necessarily all and only As are As.
5.  Necessarily all and only As are Bs.

This works for 'bachelor' and 'unmarried man'. It does not work for 'creature with a heart' and 'creature with a kidney'.

---

Note that this depends on how we understand 'necessarily'.

5.  Necessarily all material objects are material objects.
6.  Necessarily all material objects are made of protons, neutrons, and electrons.

-   If 'necessarily' means *physically necessary*, then both (5) and (6) are true. 
-  To be certain that (5) might be true and (6) false, we need a use of 'necessarily' where it means 'it is analytic that'.
-  But that makes the account circular.
